A protein bar is whey with a wrapper markup

Supplement shelves price by container and the body prices by the gram. Put every protein source on the same denominator and the ladder is unambiguous, and the top rung is a four-times markup sold as convenience.

Whey lands at 3.2 cents per gram of protein: a $58 tub holds 75 scoops at 24 grams each, 1,800 grams for the tub. Chicken breast at $3.49 a pound delivers about 100 grams of protein, 3.5 cents a gram, so the powder and the meat are effectively tied. Eggs at $4.20 a dozen run 5.8 cents. A 32-ounce tub of Greek yogurt carrying 82 grams runs 6.7. The bar is the outlier: $2.50 for 20 grams is 12.5 cents a gram, 3.9 times the whey price.

Size the daily habit and the ladder becomes a budget. A 150-gram training day built on bars is 7.5 bars, $18.75, $6,844 a year. The same target from two whey scoops and a pound of chicken, about 148 grams, is $5.04 a day, $1,838 a year. The bar version costs about $5,000 more annually for identical protein.

The markup buys processing and permission, not protein. A $2.50 bar delivers the 20 grams that 64 cents of whey delivers, and the remaining $1.86 pays for the foil, the portion control and the feeling of a snack that has been approved. Nothing inside the wrapper adds protein.

The method is three lines. Price per gram of the active ingredient, never per unit on the shelf. Compare sources on that one denominator before taste or packaging. Annualize whatever is eaten daily, because a few cents per gram compounded every day is a $5,000 year.
